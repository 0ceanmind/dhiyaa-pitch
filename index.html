Red bone marrow

Where are red cells made?

SLO 1: Structure of red bone marrow. Three slides, then the key points.

B-1.1 Structure of red bone marrow

A tissue built to make blood.

  • Highly vascular connective tissue
  • Site of hematopoiesis
  • Stem cells → all blood lineages
  • Regulated by SCF, cytokines, EPO

Red bone marrow is a highly vascular connective tissue responsible for hematopoiesis, the continuous production of blood cells.

Multipotent hematopoietic stem cells proliferate and differentiate into the blood-cell lineages, producing erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets.

Their development is regulated by growth and differentiation factors: stem cell factor (SCF), cytokines and erythropoietin.

B-1.1 Structure of red bone marrow

Structurally, red marrow consists of marrow cords separated by vascular sinusoids.

The cords are supported by reticular cells and reticulin fibers (type III collagen) and contain developing blood cells, macrophages, adipocytes, plasma cells and megakaryocytes.

Erythroid precursors form erythroblastic islands around macrophages, which support them and help regulate the maturation and release of developing red cells.

Megakaryocytes lie close to the sinusoids, so they release platelets directly into the circulation. Mature blood cells cross the sinusoidal wall to enter the bloodstream.

B-1.1 Structure of red bone marrow

Red marrow retreats with age.

Childhood

Red marrow in almost all bones

Adulthood

  • Red marrow: axial skeleton
  • Long bones → yellow (fat) marrow

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Schematic

In adults, active red marrow is located mainly in the axial skeleton, whereas much of the remaining marrow becomes yellow marrow, which is predominantly adipose tissue.

Guyton: the marrow of essentially all bones produces red cells until about age 5. After about age 20, long-bone marrow becomes fatty, except in the proximal humeri and tibiae. Adult red cells come mainly from the vertebrae, sternum, ribs and ilia.

Sources: NCBI Bookshelf (Liver) · Cullen · Krishna 2013 · Drag to rotate

The liver is organized into hepatic lobules, roughly hexagonal structural units.

Hepatocytes, the main liver cells, are arranged in plates or cords. The central vein sits in the centre and receives blood from the sinusoids. Hepatic sinusoids are specialized blood channels between the hepatocyte plates.

The portal triad sits at the corners of the lobule: a branch of the portal vein, the hepatic artery and the bile duct.

Kupffer cells are macrophages along the sinusoids that remove foreign particles and cellular debris. Bile canaliculi are small channels between hepatocytes that collect bile and direct it toward the bile ducts, opposite to blood flow. Use the Blood / Bile switch.

Source: OpenStax Biology 2e, Adaptive Immune Response · Schematic

The spleen is surrounded by a connective-tissue capsule and contains two main histological regions.

Trabeculae: extensions of connective tissue from the capsule that provide structural support.

White pulp: lymphoid tissue rich in lymphocytes. It surrounds branches of the splenic artery and is mainly involved in immune responses.

Red pulp: mainly splenic cords and sinusoids. It filters blood and helps remove old or damaged red cells.

Recycling

How are old red cells and their iron reused?

SLO 3: Roles of the liver and spleen in recycling iron and red blood cells. Four slides, then the key points.

B-1.3 Spleen and removal of old RBCs

The spleen's stress test.

Young cell bends through

≈ 8 µm cell · ≈ 3 µm gap

Old cell ruptures

Fragile, rigid membrane

Macrophages engulf it

Phagocytosis in the red pulp

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 (3 µm spaces vs ~8 µm cells)

The spleen, in the left upper abdomen, filters the blood and removes old, damaged and abnormal red cells. This happens mainly in the red pulp.

Guyton: the spaces between the red-pulp trabeculae are only about 3 µm wide, compared with an ~8 µm red cell. Young cells deform and pass; old cells, whose membranes are fragile, rupture.

Macrophages engulf and destroy the old cells by phagocytosis.

B-1.3 Iron recycling

Inside a red-pulp macrophage.

Erythrophagocytosis

Old red cells engulfed

Heme → HO-1 → Fe²⁺

Globin → amino acids

Ferroportin → transferrin

The only cellular iron exporter

Hepcidin: the switch

Binds ferroportin → degraded

Sources: Nemeth & Ganz 2021 · Slusarczyk & Mleczko-Sanecka 2021 · Schematic

Red-pulp macrophages of the spleen are a major site of iron recycling (Kupffer cells of the liver also contribute). They recognize and engulf senescent red cells: erythrophagocytosis.

Inside the macrophage, hemoglobin is split into globin (broken down to amino acids) and heme. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) degrades heme, releasing free iron.

Ferroportin, the only known cellular iron exporter, moves iron into the plasma, where it binds transferrin for delivery to the bone marrow and other tissues.

Hepcidin, a hormone from the liver, binds ferroportin and causes its internalization and degradation, trapping iron in the cell. Iron deficiency: hepcidin is suppressed, so ferroportin stays active and more recycled iron is released. Inflammation: IL-6 stimulates hepcidin, so ferroportin is degraded, iron is trapped in macrophages, and plasma iron falls (hypoferremia of anemia of inflammation).

B-1.3 Role of the liver

Two products, two destinations.

Sources: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · StatPearls, Physiology, Bilirubin

Red cells live about 120 days. Old cells are removed by Kupffer cells in the liver and by macrophages in the spleen and bone marrow.

Iron lane: hemoglobin is broken down and the iron returns to the blood. It binds transferrin and goes either to the bone marrow to make new red cells or is stored in the liver as ferritin.

Pigment lane: the porphyrin part becomes bilirubin. Unconjugated bilirubin travels on albumin, is taken up by the liver, conjugated by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (making it water-soluble) and secreted into bile.

B-1.3 In our patient

Recycling can't outrun a leak.

AVERAGE IRON LOSS

  • Loss outpaces recycling → Hb falls

Iron-loss values: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33

Closed loop: marrow → circulating red cells → macrophages → transferrin → marrow. Every red cell lost in bleeding takes its iron out of the loop.

Guyton: men lose about 0.6 mg of iron a day, mainly in feces; in women, menstrual loss brings the long-term average to about 1.3 mg a day.

Our patient: the spleen recycles iron from old red cells, but that cannot compensate for continuous iron loss. Hemoglobin production falls, leading to anemia.

Life cycle

Synthesis, breakdown, anemia and polycythemia.

SLO 4: Synthesis of red blood cells, their breakdown, and their relationship to anemia and polycythemia. Six slides, then the key points.

B-1.4 Synthesis of red blood cells

Building a red cell.

In the bone marrow · iron is essential

Low iron → smaller, paler cells · MCV 68 fL

Erythropoiesis normally occurs in the bone marrow. Hematopoietic stem cells give rise to the erythroid lineage: proerythroblasts, erythroblasts, reticulocytes and finally mature erythrocytes. Iron is essential for heme and hemoglobin synthesis during this process.

In our patient, chronic heavy menstrual bleeding has caused progressive iron loss, and her diet may also provide too little iron. As her stores become depleted, there is not enough iron for normal hemoglobin synthesis.

So the cells are smaller with less hemoglobin: MCV 68 fL, low MCH and MCHC, and a film showing microcytic, hypochromic cells with anisocytosis.

B-1.4 Synthesis of red blood cells

The kidney calls. The marrow can't answer.

  • ↓ Hb → tissue hypoxia
  • Kidney ↑ erythropoietin
  • No iron → poorly filled cells

RETICULOCYTES

0.7%

Inadequate rise

Her low hemoglobin reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood. The resulting tissue hypoxia stimulates the kidney to increase erythropoietin production, which stimulates the bone marrow to produce more red cells.

But because iron is deficient, the marrow cannot produce adequately hemoglobinized cells.

Her reticulocyte count of 0.7% therefore does not show an appropriate increase for the degree of anemia: iron-deficiency anemia from chronic blood loss, with impaired hemoglobin synthesis.

B-1.4 Breakdown of red blood cells

120 days, then recycled.

The average lifespan of a red cell is about 120 days. As cells age, their metabolic systems become progressively less active, and the membrane loses its pliability and becomes increasingly fragile.

Old cells may rupture while passing through tight areas of the circulation, especially the red pulp of the spleen. The released hemoglobin is phagocytosed by macrophages, especially Kupffer cells of the liver and macrophages of the spleen and bone marrow.

Iron is carried by transferrin to the marrow for new cells, or to the liver and other tissues for storage as ferritin. The porphyrin becomes bilirubin, which the liver secretes into bile. Drag the slider to age the cell.

B-1.4 Relationship to anemia

Anemia: too little hemoglobin.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Ring = normal 7.8 µm cell · Schematic

Definition: a deficiency of hemoglobin in the blood, caused by a reduced red cell count or a low hemoglobin content within the cells.

Acute hemorrhage: plasma is replaced (within 1–3 days), which reduces the cell concentration. Chronic hemorrhage: iron cannot be absorbed fast enough, giving small, hemoglobin-poor (microcytic, hypochromic) cells. This is our patient.

Aplastic: bone-marrow failure from radiation, chemotherapy, toxic substances or autoimmune disease; about half are idiopathic. Megaloblastic: lack of vitamin B12, folic acid or intrinsic factor (gastric mucosal atrophy, gastrectomy, intestinal malabsorption) gives large, fragile cells.

Hemolytic, e.g. hereditary spherocytosis: spherical cells rupture in the spleen. Sickle cell: HbS crystallizes when oxygen is low, sickling the cell, damaging the membrane and blocking vessels. Erythroblastosis fetalis: antibodies from an Rh-negative mother attack Rh-positive fetal cells. Severe hypochromic anemia: transferrin deficiency.

B-1.4 Relationship to polycythemia

Polycythemia: too many red cells.

Secondary

  • Response to hypoxia
  • 6–7 million/mm³

Polycythemia vera

  • Stem-cell genetic defect
  • 7–8 million/mm³
  • Hct 60–70%

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · 1 mm³ = 1 µL

Chart: red cell count in million per mm³ (1 mm³ = 1 µL, so her 4.1 × 10⁶/µL = 4.1 million/mm³). Guyton's normal values: 4.7 ± 0.3 for women, 5.2 ± 0.3 for men.

Secondary polycythemia: a physiological response to hypoxia, e.g. high altitude or heart failure; count rises to 6–7 million/mm³.

Polycythemia vera (erythremia): a genetic defect in stem cells causes excessive, continuous production; count 7–8 million/mm³, hematocrit 60–70%.

B-1.4 Effects on the circulatory system

Thin blood, thick blood.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Viscosity relative to water

Anemia: blood viscosity drops to about 1.5 times water (instead of about 3). Lower resistance plus hypoxic vasodilation raise cardiac output to 3–4 times normal. Cardiac strain: severe hypoxia and heart failure during exertion.

Polycythemia vera: total blood volume can double and viscosity rises to about 10 times water. Flow is sluggish because of the high resistance, but cardiac output stays stable because the high viscosity is balanced by the doubled blood volume. Hypertension in about one-third of patients. Skin: ruddy complexion with a bluish (cyanotic) tint from congestion, slow flow and more deoxygenated hemoglobin.

The numbers

What does the blood count tell us?

SLO 5: Relate hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW and RBC count. Six slides, then the key points.

B-1.5 Hemoglobin & hematocrit

Three numbers, three questions.

RBC countHow many cells?
Hematocrit% of blood volume
HemoglobinO₂-carrying protein

Related, not interchangeable.

Red cells carry oxygen through hemoglobin, the major protein inside them, so measuring hemoglobin is important when assessing anemia.

Hematocrit is the percentage of blood volume occupied by red cells: a hematocrit of 45% means about 45% of blood volume is red cells. The RBC count is the number of red cells in a given volume of blood.

They are related but not interchangeable: two people can have the same RBC count, but larger cells occupy more volume and give a higher hematocrit. The count can also be the same while hemoglobin is lower.

B-1.5 MCV, MCH & MCHC

Inside the average red cell.

MCV (mean corpuscular volume): the average size of red cells. Low = microcytic, normal = normocytic, high = macrocytic. Especially useful for classifying anemia by cell size.

MCH (mean corpuscular hemoglobin): the average amount of hemoglobin in one cell. MCHC: the average concentration of hemoglobin inside the cells; low = hypochromic.

Together: MCV is size, MCH is the amount of hemoglobin per cell, MCHC is its concentration. Patient: MCV 68 fL, MCH 21 pg, MCHC 29 g/dL, all low. Guyton gives the normal cell volume as about 90–95 µm³ (fL).

B-1.5 Red cell distribution width

RDW: how different are the sizes?

MCVAverage size
RDWSize variation

Our patient

Blood films are schematic

RDW measures the variation in the size of red cells. High RDW: the cells have different sizes (anisocytosis). Normal RDW: cells are similar in size.

RDW differs from MCV: MCV tells how big the cells are on average, RDW how different their sizes are.

Our patient has a low MCV and a high RDW: her cells are smaller than normal and vary significantly in size.

B-1.5 RDW and types of anemia

Read RDW with MCV.

PatternPossible condition
↓ MCV + ↑ RDWIron-deficiency anemia Our patient
↓ MCV + normal RDWThalassemia trait
↑ MCV + ↑ RDWB12 / folate deficiency
  • RDW alone can't diagnose · iron studies confirm

Source: Warner & Kamran, StatPearls, Iron Deficiency Anemia

Low MCV + high RDW: commonly iron-deficiency anemia (our patient). Low MCV + normal RDW: can be seen in thalassemia trait. High MCV + high RDW: can occur in vitamin B12/folate deficiency.

However, RDW alone cannot diagnose the cause of anemia. Interpret it with the other CBC findings and the clinical history. Iron studies are needed to confirm iron deficiency.

B-1.5 A simple way to remember

Four questions for any CBC.

Hb / Hct→Is there anemia?
MCV→What size are the cells?
MCH / MCHC→How much hemoglobin?
RDW→How different are the sizes?

The indices are interpreted together, not individually. Hb/Hct: is there anemia? MCV: what size? MCH/MCHC: how much hemoglobin? RDW: how different are the sizes? RBC count: how many cells.

The molecules

What changes when iron runs out?

SLO 6: Biochemical changes in microcytic anemia due to iron deficiency (iron and ferritin binding, transport, amino acids, enzymes, vitamins). Six slides, then the key points.

B-1.6 Iron metabolism

Iron in, iron out.

Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ → DMT1

Absorbed in the duodenum

Store or export

Ferritin · ferroportin

Transferrin carries it

To marrow, or to stores

Hepcidin sets the flow

High hepcidin → less iron out

Sources: StatPearls, Iron Absorption · Nemeth & Ganz 2021 · Schematic

Iron is an essential mineral; the body keeps iron balance mainly through recycling and intestinal absorption. Dietary iron is absorbed mainly in the duodenum. Non-heme iron is reduced to Fe²⁺ and enters intestinal cells mainly through DMT1.

Inside the cell, iron is either stored as ferritin or exported into the blood through ferroportin. In blood it binds transferrin, which delivers it to tissues, mainly the bone marrow for red-cell production. Iron is also stored in tissues as ferritin.

Hepcidin from the liver acts on ferroportin. High hepcidin reduces ferroportin, so less iron enters the blood. In iron deficiency, hepcidin decreases, so more iron is absorbed and released from stores. In our patient, prolonged heavy bleeding meant losses exceeded absorption for a long time, so stores became depleted.

B-1.6 Iron metabolism

Where the body keeps its iron.

  • ≈ ⅔ inside hemoglobin
  • Balance: recycling + absorption
  • Loss empties ferritin stores first

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Stores are 15–30% of body iron

Guyton: total body iron averages 4–5 g: about 65% in hemoglobin, 4% in myoglobin, 1% in heme compounds that promote intracellular oxidation, 0.1% bound to transferrin in plasma, and 15–30% stored, mainly as ferritin in the reticuloendothelial system and liver.

The body maintains iron balance mainly through recycling and absorption. If iron loss continues for a long time and exceeds replacement, the stores are depleted, and then hemoglobin synthesis suffers.

B-1.6 Enzymes, amino acids, vitamins

No iron, no heme.

Sources: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · StatPearls, Biochemistry, Heme Synthesis

Heme is the iron-containing part of hemoglobin. Its synthesis starts with glycine (an amino acid) and succinyl-CoA, which ALA synthase, with vitamin B6 as cofactor, converts to ALA. After several steps, protoporphyrin IX forms. In the final step, ferrochelatase inserts Fe²⁺ into protoporphyrin IX to make heme. Heme then combines with globin chains to form hemoglobin.

In iron deficiency, less Fe²⁺ is available for that final step, so less heme and less hemoglobin are made. Cells contain less hemoglobin (hypochromic) and are smaller (microcytic): this explains her low MCV, MCH and MCHC.

Guyton: when hemoglobin formation is deficient, cells hold less hemoglobin and their volume may also fall, because there is less hemoglobin to fill them.

B-1.6 From heme to symptoms

Four irons. Four oxygens.

4 chains · 4 hemes

Each iron binds one O₂

1 g Hb ≈ 1.34 mL O₂

≈ 36% less O₂ capacity

→ her fatigue, breathlessness, tachycardia

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Patient: 8.9 × 1.34 · Schematic

Guyton: each hemoglobin molecule has four globin chains, each with a heme group holding one iron atom; each iron binds one O₂ molecule. Each gram of hemoglobin binds about 1.34 mL of O₂.

A normal woman (about 14 g/dL) carries about 19 mL O₂ per 100 mL of blood. Our patient: 8.9 × 1.34 ≈ 11.9 mL, about 36% less. That explains her fatigue, dizziness, shortness of breath, palpitations, pallor and tachycardia.

Red bone marrow

Where are red cells made, and what does the factory look like?

SLO 1: Structure of red bone marrow.

B-1.1 Structure of red bone marrow

A tissue built to make blood.

Red bone marrow is a highly vascular connective tissue responsible for hematopoiesis, the continuous production of blood cells.

Multipotent hematopoietic stem cells proliferate and differentiate into the blood-cell lineages.

Their development is regulated by stem cell factor (SCF), cytokines and erythropoietin.

Red marrow = highly vascular connective tissue; job = hematopoiesis, the continuous production of blood cells.

Multipotent hematopoietic stem cells proliferate and differentiate into all lineages: erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets.

Regulators: stem cell factor, cytokines, erythropoietin.

B-1.1 Structure of red bone marrow

Inside the marrow.

Architecture

Cords between sinusoids

Marrow cords of developing cells are separated by wide vascular sinusoids.

Support

A reticular scaffold

Reticular cells and reticulin fibers (type III collagen) support the cords, which hold developing blood cells, macrophages, adipocytes, plasma cells and megakaryocytes.

Erythroblastic island

A macrophage nursery

Erythroid precursors cluster around a central macrophage, which supports them and helps regulate their maturation and release.

Megakaryocyte

Platelets, straight into blood

Megakaryocytes lie beside the sinusoids, so they release platelets directly into the circulation.

Release

Crossing the wall

Mature blood cells cross the sinusoidal wall to enter the bloodstream.

Drag the model to rotate · Schematic, not to scale

Step through: cords and sinusoids, then reticular cells and reticulin (type III collagen), then the erythroblastic island around a macrophage, then megakaryocytes beside sinusoids shedding platelets, then mature cells crossing the sinusoid wall.

You can drag the 3D model to rotate it.

B-1.1 Structure of red bone marrow

Red marrow retreats with age.

Childhood

Until about 5 years of age, the marrow of essentially all bones produces red cells.

Adulthood

Active red marrow lies mainly in the axial skeleton: vertebrae, sternum, ribs and ilia. Most of the remaining marrow becomes yellow marrow, which is mostly adipose tissue.

After about age 20, long-bone marrow becomes fatty, except in the proximal humeri and tibiae.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology, Ch. 33 · Schematic skeleton

In adults, active red marrow is mainly in the axial skeleton; much of the rest becomes yellow (adipose) marrow.

Guyton: all bones produce red cells until about age 5; after about 20, long-bone marrow (except proximal humeri and tibiae) is fatty; adult red cells come mainly from vertebrae, sternum, ribs and ilia.

SLO 2: Histological structure of the liver and spleen.

B-1.2 Histology of the liver

The hepatic lobule.

Structural unit

Roughly hexagonal

The liver is organized into hepatic lobules, roughly hexagonal structural units.

Hepatocytes

Plates of liver cells

The main liver cells, arranged in plates or cords.

Portal triad

Three vessels at each corner

A branch of the portal vein, the hepatic artery and the bile duct.

Sinusoids → central vein

Blood flows inward

Specialized blood channels between the plates drain into the central vein at the center of the lobule.

Kupffer cells

Macrophages on guard

They line the sinusoids and remove foreign particles and cellular debris.

Bile canaliculi

Bile flows outward

Small channels between hepatocytes collect bile and direct it toward the bile ducts, the opposite direction to blood.

Sources: NCBI Bookshelf, Anatomy, Abdomen and Pelvis: Liver · Cullen, normal liver histology · Krishna, Clin Liver Dis 2013 · Drag to rotate

Hexagonal lobule. Hepatocytes in plates. Portal triad at the corners: portal vein branch, hepatic artery, bile duct. Sinusoids carry blood to the central vein. Kupffer cells line the sinusoids. Bile canaliculi carry bile toward the bile ducts.

B-1.2 Histology of the spleen

The spleen.

Overview

A capsule and two kinds of pulp

The spleen is surrounded by a connective-tissue capsule and contains two main histological regions.

Trabeculae

Internal scaffolding

Extensions of connective tissue from the capsule that provide structural support.

White pulp

The immune tissue

Lymphoid tissue rich in lymphocytes. It surrounds branches of the splenic artery and is mainly involved in immune responses.

Red pulp

The blood filter

Mainly splenic cords and sinusoids. It filters blood and helps remove old or damaged red cells.

Source: OpenStax Biology, Adaptive Immune Response · Schematic section

Capsule of connective tissue; trabeculae extend inward for support.

White pulp: lymphocyte-rich lymphoid tissue around branches of the splenic artery, mainly immune.

Red pulp: splenic cords and sinusoids; filters blood and removes old or damaged red cells. This is where SLO 3 picks up.

The recycling plant

How the liver and spleen recycle old red cells and their iron.

SLO 3: Roles of the liver and spleen in recycling iron and red blood cells.

B-1.3 Spleen and removal of old RBCs

The spleen's stress test.

In the red pulp, red cells about 8 µm across must squeeze through spaces only about 3 µm wide.

Young cell: bends and passes

A flexible membrane lets it fold through the slit.

Old cell: fragile, it ruptures

Ageing membranes lose pliability. Old, damaged and abnormal cells are filtered out.

Phagocytosis

Red-pulp macrophages engulf and destroy the old cells, then recycle what is inside.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 (3 µm spaces vs ~8 µm cells) · Animation not to scale in time

The spleen, in the left upper abdomen, filters blood and removes old, damaged and abnormal red cells, mainly in the red pulp.

Guyton: spaces between red-pulp trabeculae are only about 3 µm wide, versus an ~8 µm red cell. Young cells deform and pass; fragile old cells rupture and are phagocytosed by macrophages.

B-1.3 Iron recycling

Inside a red-pulp macrophage.

1 · Erythrophagocytosis

Engulfing old cells

Red-pulp macrophages of the spleen, a major site of iron recycling, recognize and engulf senescent red cells.

2 · Breakdown

Freeing the iron

Hemoglobin splits into globin (to amino acids) and heme. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) degrades heme, releasing free iron.

3 · Export

Through ferroportin

Ferroportin, the only known cellular iron exporter, moves iron into plasma, where it binds transferrin for delivery to the bone marrow.

4 · The master switch

Hepcidin from the liver

Hepcidin binds ferroportin, causing its internalization and degradation.

Sources: Nemeth & Ganz, Int J Mol Sci 2021 (hepcidin–ferroportin) · Slusarczyk & Mleczko-Sanecka, Genes 2021 (iron recycling) · Schematic

A major site of iron recycling: red-pulp macrophages of the spleen (Kupffer cells of the liver also contribute). Erythrophagocytosis, then HO-1 degrades heme, releasing iron.

Ferroportin is the only known cellular iron exporter. In plasma, iron binds transferrin for delivery to the marrow.

Hepcidin (from the liver) binds ferroportin → internalization and degradation → iron trapped. Iron deficiency: hepcidin suppressed, more iron released. Inflammation: IL-6 raises hepcidin → hypoferremia of anemia of inflammation.

B-1.3 Role of the liver

Two products, two destinations.

After about 120 days, old red cells are removed by Kupffer cells in the liver and by macrophages in the spleen and bone marrow.

Sources: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · StatPearls, Physiology, Bilirubin

Hemoglobin is broken down. Iron returns to blood, binds transferrin, and goes either to the marrow for new red cells or is stored in the liver as ferritin.

The porphyrin part becomes bilirubin. Unconjugated bilirubin travels on albumin, is taken up by the liver, conjugated by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (more water-soluble) and secreted into bile.

B-1.3 In our patient

Recycling can't outrun a leak.

AVERAGE LONG-TERM IRON LOSS

The spleen recycles iron from old cells, but recycling cannot compensate for continuous blood loss. Hemoglobin production falls, and anemia follows.

Iron-loss values: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33

Closed loop: marrow → circulating red cells → macrophages → transferrin → marrow. Every red cell lost in bleeding takes its iron out of the loop.

Guyton: men lose about 0.6 mg iron/day; in women, menstrual loss brings the long-term average to about 1.3 mg/day. Our patient's bleeding is heavy and prolonged.

Made, used, destroyed

Synthesis and breakdown of red cells, and what happens when the balance fails: anemia and polycythemia.

SLO 4: Synthesis of red cells, their breakdown, and their relationship to anemia and polycythemia.

B-1.4 Synthesis of red blood cells

Building a red cell.

Erythropoiesis normally occurs in the bone marrow. Iron is essential for heme and hemoglobin synthesis along the way.

Without enough iron, the cells come out smaller with less hemoglobin: MCV 68 fL, low MCH and MCHC.

Hematopoietic stem cell → proerythroblast → erythroblasts → reticulocyte → mature erythrocyte. Hemoglobin accumulates as the cell matures; the nucleus is lost.

Patient: chronic menstrual loss plus low dietary iron → stores depleted → not enough iron for hemoglobin synthesis → smaller, paler cells (MCV 68 fL, low MCH and MCHC; film microcytic, hypochromic, anisocytosis).

B-1.4 Synthesis of red blood cells

The kidney calls. The marrow can't answer.

Low hemoglobin reduces oxygen-carrying capacity. Tissue hypoxia makes the kidney increase erythropoietin, which stimulates the marrow.

But with iron deficient, the marrow cannot make adequately hemoglobinized cells.

RETICULOCYTE COUNT

0.7%

Not the rise expected for this degree of anemia.

Low Hb → less O2 carried → tissue hypoxia → kidney raises erythropoietin → marrow stimulated.

But iron is deficient, so the marrow cannot make adequately hemoglobinized cells. Reticulocyte count 0.7% does not show an appropriate increase for the degree of anemia.

B-1.4 Breakdown of red blood cells

120 days, then recycled.

Average lifespan ~120 days. With age, metabolic systems become less active, the membrane loses pliability and becomes fragile.

Old cells rupture in tight spots, especially the red pulp of the spleen. Released hemoglobin is phagocytosed, especially by Kupffer cells and by macrophages of spleen and marrow.

Iron → transferrin → marrow (new cells) or liver/tissues as ferritin. Porphyrin → bilirubin → secreted by the liver into bile.

B-1.4 Relationship to anemia

Anemia.

A deficiency of hemoglobin in the blood, from too few red cells or too little hemoglobin in each.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · 3D cells are schematic

Blood loss: acute (plasma is replaced, diluting the red cells) and chronic (iron cannot be absorbed fast enough → microcytic hypochromic cells, our patient).

Aplastic: marrow failure from radiation, chemotherapy, toxic substances or autoimmune disease; about half idiopathic.

Megaloblastic: lack of B12, folic acid or intrinsic factor (gastric mucosal atrophy, gastrectomy, malabsorption) → large, fragile cells.

Hemolytic, e.g. hereditary spherocytosis: spherical cells rupture in the spleen. Sickle cell: HbS crystallizes at low O2 → sickle shape, membrane damage, vessel blockage.

Erythroblastosis fetalis: antibodies from an Rh-negative mother attack Rh-positive fetal cells. Severe hypochromic anemia: transferrin deficiency.

B-1.4 Relationship to polycythemia

Polycythemia: too many red cells.

Secondary polycythemia

A physiological response to hypoxia, such as high altitude or heart failure. Count rises to 6–7 million/mm³.

Polycythemia vera (erythremia)

A genetic defect in stem cells causes excessive, continuous production. Count 7–8 million/mm³, hematocrit 60–70%.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · 1 mm³ = 1 µL, so 4.1 × 10⁶/µL = 4.1 million/mm³

Chart: red cell count in million per mm³. Our patient 4.1; Guyton's normal values are 4.7 ± 0.3 for women and 5.2 ± 0.3 for men.

Secondary polycythemia (hypoxia: altitude, heart failure) 6–7 million/mm³. Polycythemia vera, a stem-cell genetic defect: 7–8 million/mm³, hematocrit 60–70%.

B-1.4 Effects on the circulatory system

Thin blood, thick blood.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Viscosity relative to water

Severe anemia: viscosity falls to ~1.5× water (normal ~3×). Lower resistance plus hypoxic vasodilation raise cardiac output up to 3–4× normal. On exertion: severe hypoxia and heart failure.

Polycythemia vera: total blood volume can double and viscosity rises to ~10× water. Flow is sluggish, but cardiac output stays near normal because the high viscosity is balanced by the bigger blood volume. Hypertension in about one-third. Ruddy complexion with a bluish (cyanotic) tint.

SLO 5: Relate hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW and RBC count.

B-1.5 Hemoglobin & hematocrit

Three numbers, three questions.

RBC count

How many red cells are in a volume of blood?

Hematocrit (Hct)

What percentage of blood volume do they occupy?

Hemoglobin (Hb)

How much oxygen-carrying protein is present?

Related, but not interchangeable.

RBC count = number of red cells in a volume of blood. Hematocrit = percentage of blood volume occupied by red cells (Hct 45% → about 45% of blood is red cells). Hemoglobin = the oxygen-carrying protein, so it reflects oxygen-carrying capacity.

Not interchangeable: two people can have the same RBC count, but larger cells give a higher hematocrit, and the count can be the same while hemoglobin is lower.

B-1.5 MCV, MCH & MCHC

Inside the average red cell.

MCV, mean corpuscular volume, is the average size: low = microcytic, normal = normocytic, high = macrocytic. It classifies anemia by cell size.

MCH, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, is the average amount of hemoglobin in one cell. MCHC is the average concentration of hemoglobin inside cells: low = hypochromic.

Patient: MCV 68 fL, MCH 21 pg, MCHC 29 g/dL, all low. Guyton gives the normal cell volume as about 90–95 µm³ (fL).

B-1.5 Red cell distribution width

RDW: how different are the sizes?

MCV

How big the cells are on average.

RDW

How much their sizes vary.

High RDW means the cells differ in size: anisocytosis. Our patient: RDW 18% ↑

Blood films are schematic

RDW measures variation in red cell size. High RDW = anisocytosis; normal RDW = cells similar in size.

MCV = average size; RDW = variation in size. Patient RDW 18% (high) with low MCV: cells are small and vary a lot in size.

Source: Warner & Kamran, StatPearls, Iron Deficiency Anemia

Low MCV + high RDW: commonly iron deficiency (our patient). Low MCV + normal RDW: can be seen in thalassemia trait. High MCV + high RDW: can occur in B12/folate deficiency.

Caveat: RDW alone cannot diagnose; interpret with the full CBC and history; iron studies confirm.

B-1.5 A simple way to remember

Four questions to ask any CBC.

Hb / Hct→Is there anemia?
MCV→What size are the red cells?
MCH / MCHC→How much hemoglobin do they have?
RDW→How different are their sizes?

The RBC indices are interpreted together, not individually. Hb/Hct: is there anemia? MCV: what size? MCH/MCHC: how much hemoglobin? RDW: how different are the sizes? RBC count: how many cells.

B-1.6 Iron metabolism

Iron in, iron out.

Absorption · duodenum

Reduce, then enter

Non-heme iron is reduced to Fe²⁺ and enters intestinal cells mainly through the transporter DMT1.

Inside the enterocyte

Store or export

Iron is either stored as ferritin or exported into the blood through ferroportin.

Transport

Carried by transferrin

In blood, iron binds transferrin and travels to the bone marrow for red-cell production, or to tissues for storage as ferritin.

Regulation

Hepcidin sets the flow

High hepcidin reduces ferroportin, so less iron enters the blood.

Sources: StatPearls, Biochemistry, Iron Absorption · Nemeth & Ganz, Int J Mol Sci 2021 · Schematic

Dietary iron is absorbed mainly in the duodenum. Non-heme iron is reduced to Fe2+ and enters via DMT1. In the cell it is stored as ferritin or exported by ferroportin. In blood it binds transferrin and goes to the marrow or is stored as ferritin.

Hepcidin from the liver acts on ferroportin. High hepcidin: less iron released. Iron deficiency: hepcidin falls, so more iron is absorbed and released from stores. In our patient, chronic blood loss outpaced absorption and stores became depleted.

B-1.6 Iron metabolism

Where the body keeps its iron.

Total body iron averages 4–5 g. About two-thirds of it sits inside hemoglobin.

Balance is kept mainly by recycling and by adjusting intestinal absorption.

When loss keeps exceeding replacement, the ferritin stores are spent first, then hemoglobin synthesis suffers.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · Stores are 15–30% of body iron

Guyton: body iron 4–5 g: about 65% in hemoglobin, 4% myoglobin, 1% heme compounds for intracellular oxidation, 0.1% bound to transferrin in plasma, and 15–30% stored, mainly as ferritin.

The body keeps balance mainly by recycling and absorption. If loss continues for a long time and exceeds replacement, stores are depleted.

B-1.6 Heme synthesis: enzymes, amino acids, vitamins

No iron, no heme.

Sources: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 · StatPearls, Biochemistry, Heme Synthesis

Heme synthesis starts with glycine (an amino acid) and succinyl-CoA. ALA synthase, with vitamin B6 as cofactor, makes ALA. After several steps, protoporphyrin IX forms. Ferrochelatase inserts Fe2+ to make heme. Heme plus globin chains = hemoglobin.

In iron deficiency, less Fe2+ is available for the last step → less heme → less hemoglobin → cells hypochromic and microcytic: low MCV, MCH, MCHC.

B-1.6 From heme to symptoms

Four irons. Four oxygens.

One hemoglobin molecule

4 globin chains, each with a heme group holding one iron atom. Each iron can bind one O₂ molecule.

Capacity follows hemoglobin

Each gram of hemoglobin binds about 1.34 mL of O₂.

That is the fatigue

About 36% less oxygen-carrying capacity explains her fatigue, dizziness, shortness of breath, palpitations, pallor and tachycardia.

Source: Guyton & Hall, Ch. 33 (1.34 mL O₂/g Hb; women average 14 g Hb/dL) · Patient value calculated: 8.9 × 1.34 · 3D model schematic

Guyton: each hemoglobin has four heme groups, each with an iron atom; each binds one O2 molecule. 1 g Hb binds about 1.34 mL O2.

A normal woman (about 14 g/dL) carries about 19 mL O2 per 100 mL blood. Our patient: 8.9 × 1.34 ≈ 11.9 mL, about 36% less. That explains the symptoms.

B-1.6 Case correlation

Iron studies confirm it.

Source: Warner & Kamran, StatPearls, Iron Deficiency Anemia · Transferrin carriers are schematic

Her iron studies are typical of iron deficiency: low ferritin (depleted stores), low serum iron, high TIBC (more unfilled iron-binding capacity) and low transferrin saturation.

Together: depleted iron stores and reduced iron availability for red-cell production.
